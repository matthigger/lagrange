Lagrange multipliers, visually

Walk along a constraint: at its best points, the gradient of f lines up with the gradient of the constraint.

Optimize along a path: where ∇f = λ∇g comes from

Problem: find the max or min of f(x) while staying on the path g(x) = 0. Setting ∇f = 0 doesn't work: at the best point on the path, ∇f is usually not 0, since stepping off the path could still raise f. Here f(x) = x0x1 and the path is the unit circle, g(x) = x02 + x12 − 1 = 0.

Approach: walk along the path and ask when no step along it raises f.

  1. Walking in a unit direction u changes f at rate u · ∇f. Let u point along the path: that rate is the red arrow, and the slope of the plot on the right.
  2. If u · ∇f ≠ 0, f rises one way along the path and falls the other, so x is not a max or a min. At a constrained max or min, ∇f ⊥ path.
  3. ∇g ⊥ path everywhere on it: g stays 0 along the path, so u · ∇g = 0.

In 2D, two vectors perpendicular to the same path are parallel:

∇f(x) = λ ∇g(x) g(x) = 0

Three equations (two rows of the first, and the constraint) in three unknowns: x0, x1 and the multiplier λ (Lagrange 1788). Each row of the first asks for a λ; the readout shows both, and they agree only where ∇f and ∇g are parallel.

Try this

  • Drag the point around the circle. Where does the red arrow vanish? Find all four places.
    AnswerAt (±1/√2, ±1/√2). With x0, x1 the same sign they are maxima (f = 1/2); with opposite signs, minima (f = −1/2). At each one the level set of f through the point touches the circle without crossing it.
  • Press Climb from a few starting points. Where does it stop, and why there?
    AnswerClimb steps along the red arrow, so it stops exactly where the red arrow is 0: where ∇f has no part along the path. That is a constrained max (a min, for Descend).
  • Turn on Level sets of g. What angle does ∇g make with them?
    AnswerA right angle, always: g doesn't change along its own level set, so ∇g has no part along it. The circle is the level set g = 0.
  • Compare the two rows' λ at a max, then at a min. Why is λ negative at a min?
    AnswerAt a max f grows outward, the way g grows, so ∇f and ∇g point the same way (λ = 1/2). At a min f grows inward (λ = −1/2). Writing the constraint as 1 − x02 − x12 = 0 would flip every sign, so for an equality the sign alone means nothing. For an inequality it does: see the last example on In practice.
  • Switch to 3D and follow the path lifted onto the surface.
    AnswerIts high and low points are the answers. The surface is a saddle: off the path, f has no max or min at all.
